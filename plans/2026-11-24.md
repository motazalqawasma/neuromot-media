## Why this one
- Strong, simple hook with a twist: the first way to see the brain on an X-ray was to replace the brain's fluid with AIR.
- Clear clinical point for a 3D zoom: the ventricles (fluid spaces) show up as dark shapes when filled with air (negative contrast); a tumour pushes them out of shape.
- Series feel after Godlee (Sunday): "In 1884 they had no scans. 34 years later, this man found a trick."
- Real photos exist (Dandy portrait; a historical pneumoencephalogram on Commons).

## Facts (STEP 1 must re-check each with 2+ sources; use only what checks out)
1. Walter E. Dandy (1886-1946), Johns Hopkins neurosurgeon, trained under Halsted (chief resident from 1916). (Wikipedia; Embryo Project ASU 2017)
2. 1918: "Ventriculography following the injection of air into the cerebral ventricles", Annals of Surgery 1918;68:5-11. (Garrison-Morton historyofmedicine.com id 8927; ASU; American Scientist 2023)
3. 1919: air injected through the spinal canal (pneumoencephalography), Annals of Surgery 1919;70:397-403 (ASU; check second source).
4. Idea: as a general surgeon he knew free air under the diaphragm shows clearly on X-ray (Wikipedia: free air outlines the abdominal contents; American Scientist: a radiolucent region under the diaphragm). X-rays pass more easily through air than tissue = negative contrast (American Scientist).
5. Used as the main way to localise brain lesions until CT in the 1970s (Wikipedia; ASU; American Scientist).
6. The cost: bad headaches, nausea, vomiting (American Scientist 2023: "At minimum..."). The 30% mortality line is from one source only and unclear (early series?): do NOT use unless a second source confirms.
7. Extra (optional, 1 line): Dandy also did the first intracranial aneurysm clipping (1937; ASU) - Wikipedia gives 1937 and 1938, so say "1937" only if a second source agrees, else drop.
