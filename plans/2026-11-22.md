Anniversary hook: the operation was 25 November 1884, so Sunday is 3 days before its 142nd anniversary.

## Why this one
- No scans, no X-rays (X-rays came in 1895): a neurologist found the tumour only from the patient's signs, told the surgeon where to cut, and he was right.
- Clear clinical lesson that fits a 3D zoom: motor cortex map (the twitching hand points to the opposite precentral gyrus), the same idea as Penfield but 50 years earlier.
- Strong twist: the tumour came out cleanly, but the patient died 28 days later of infection (meningitis).

## Facts (night run must re-check each with 2+ sources; use only what checks out)
1. 25 November 1884, Hospital for Epilepsy and Paralysis, Regent's Park, London. Surgeon Rickman J. Godlee; neurologist Alexander Hughes Bennett localised it from neurological signs alone. (J Neurosurg 1984;61(5):809-13, doi:10.3171/jns.1984.61.5.0809, "The first primary brain-tumor operation"; Wikipedia Rickman Godlee as pointer)
2. Tumour = glioma. Autopsy found no remnant of the removed tumour. Death on the 28th day after surgery from "apparent meningitis and secondary complications". (J Neurosurg 1984 abstract)
3. Patient details often told: Scottish farmer, about 25, seizures/twitching starting in one side of the face/hand, then weakness. RE-CHECK (name "Henderson", age, side) with a full-text source before using any of it; if not confirmed, say only "a young man".
4. Godlee was Joseph Lister's nephew (antiseptic surgery) - check; nice line if true.
5. Clinical point: the precentral gyrus (motor cortex) controls the opposite side of the body; a seizure that starts in one hand points to that spot (motor homunculus). Cite Gray's/StatPearls.
